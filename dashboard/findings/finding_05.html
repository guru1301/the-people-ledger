SECTION 2 · UNDERSTANDING RESERVED CONSTITUENCIES Constitutional framework

In the Indian electoral system, specific constituencies are reserved for Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) to ensure proportional political representation as mandated by the Constitution.

In General constituencies, any eligible citizen can contest the election. In SC and ST reserved constituencies, only candidates belonging to the respective Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes are permitted to file nominations and contest.

It is critical to note that while reservation determines who can contest the election, it does not restrict who can vote. The entire electorate of that constituency, regardless of their community, votes to elect their representative. This system aims to provide adequate political voice while maintaining universal adult suffrage.

SECTION 8 · INVESTIGATIVE REPORT Long-form analysis of election data

Closer Contests in Reserved Constituencies: What the Numbers Show

The architectural foundation of Tamil Nadu's electoral landscape is structured around 234 assembly constituencies, subdivided into General and Reserved categories. When the final ballots were counted in 2026, the election results highlighted an intriguing pattern of electoral competitiveness. By isolating winning margins across these categories, the data describes a scenario where victory in SC-reserved constituencies often required a narrower electoral advantage than in General constituencies.

Understanding electoral competitiveness requires looking past total vote shares to examine the space between the winner and the runner-up. The winning margin serves as the definitive metric for how tightly fought an election truly was. A small margin indicates a highly competitive environment where voters were closely divided between top candidates, whereas a large margin points to a decisive, unilateral mandate.

Within Tamil Nadu's 234 seats, the distribution stands at 188 General constituencies, 44 Scheduled Caste (SC) constituencies, and 2 Scheduled Tribe (ST) constituencies. The data reveals that the average winning margin across all 188 General constituencies stood at 17,543.85 votes. In contrast, the 44 SC-reserved constituencies recorded a lower average winning margin of 14,191.50 votes. This represents a substantial average difference of 3,352 votes.

However, averages in elections can often be skewed by outliers. A single candidate winning by a monumental landslide of 60,000 votes in one constituency can artificially inflate the average for that entire category. To account for this, examining the median margin—the exact midpoint where half the margins are higher and half are lower—provides a more robust measure of a "typical" contest.

The median data confirms the pattern suggested by the averages. The median winning margin for General constituencies was 11,710 votes, while the median margin for SC constituencies was 10,097 votes. The fact that both the mean and median margins were narrower in SC-reserved constituencies reinforces the observation that these seats tended to experience closer electoral races during this election cycle.

Beyond just the margins, the distribution of victories among major alliances also varied across these categories. The leading TVK alliance secured 84 out of the 188 General constituencies, amounting to a 44.68% win rate in that category. However, their performance in SC-reserved constituencies was proportionately stronger, winning 23 out of 44 seats for a 52.27% win rate. The numbers indicate that TVK won a higher proportion of SC-reserved constituencies than General constituencies, effectively capturing a majority of the seats in that specific category.

When analyzing this data, it is imperative to address the ST-reserved constituencies. Tamil Nadu has only two ST constituencies: Yercaud (AC 83) in Salem district and Senthamangalam (AC 93) in Namakkal district. The average winning margin for these two seats was 2,422 votes, and TVK won one of them (50%). However, because this represents a sample size of exactly two, no statewide statistical inferences can be drawn. The data for ST constituencies is descriptive of those specific localities only and cannot be used to establish broader trends.

The verified election returns present a clear empirical picture: SC-reserved constituencies recorded smaller winning margins than General constituencies in 2026. This finding provides an objective lens into the mechanics of the state's electoral competitiveness, illustrating exactly how narrow the path to victory was in different parts of the electoral map.

SECTION 10 · LIMITATIONS Important contextual boundaries

The observed differences in electoral competitiveness describe the outcome of the 2026 election, but they DO NOT imply causation. Reservation status alone does not explain electoral competitiveness.

Electoral margins are influenced by a complex web of factors including candidate selection, party strength, local political dynamics, regional issues, and campaign strategies. The data shows that SC constituencies had closer margins, but it does not claim that the reservation policy itself caused these closer races.

Furthermore, because only two ST constituencies exist in the state, ST analysis remains strictly descriptive rather than statistically significant.

CONCLUSION

Finding #5: Electoral Competitiveness Varied Across Reservation Categories

SC Reserved constituencies recorded lower average and median winning margins than General constituencies. TVK secured a higher proportion of victories in SC seats. These findings describe patterns in the 2026 election and should not be interpreted as evidence that reservation status caused closer contests.

"Tamil Nadu's 2026 election reveals that electoral competitiveness differed across constituency categories. In many SC-reserved constituencies, victory required a narrower margin, illustrating how closely contested these races were—but the numbers describe the outcome, not the reason behind it."
